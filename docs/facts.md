# Facts used in the game

Each fact shown to players, with where to check it. Sources: Australian Museum (AM), WA Museum (WAM), CSIRO, Atlas of Living Australia (ALA), and published research where noted. Check before changing any text in `js/missions.js`.

## Bull ant (*Myrmecia*)
- Almost all bull ant species are endemic to Australia (one in New Caledonia). AM.
- Large eyes, hunt visually and usually alone. AM.
- Workers are all female; the queen lays eggs. AM.
- Venomous, painful sting. AM.
- Adults drink nectar/sweet liquids; larvae are fed insect prey. AM.
- Echidnas eat ants and termites, dig into nests. AM.
- Ants carry prey heavier than themselves. General.

## Bull ant queen stage
- Colonies are founded by a single queen. AM.
- Young queens (and males) are winged; workers are wingless. AM.
- Queens are larger than workers and lay the eggs. AM.
- Nuptial flights happen on warm days, often after rain. AM; general ant biology.
- After mating, queens remove their wings. General ant biology (Hölldobler & Wilson, *The Ants*).
- Queen digs a founding chamber and lays her first eggs. Hölldobler & Wilson.
- *Myrmecia* founding queens are semi-claustral: they leave the nest to forage for their first brood; many other ants found claustrally (sealed in). Hölldobler & Wilson; Haskins & Haskins.
- Once workers emerge they forage; the queen lays eggs for years. General.
- Willie wagtail (*Rhipidura leucophrys*) takes flying insects and forages on the ground. BirdLife Australia.

## Bush fly (*Musca vetustissima*)
- Bush flies seek moisture and protein from sweat, tears, saliva and other secretions; hence flies on faces. CSIRO.
- Females need protein to mature eggs; they feed on animal secretions (eyes, nose, wounds) of cattle and other animals. CSIRO; Hughes et al.
- Flies have sponging mouthparts and cannot bite or chew (muscid house/bush flies). AM.
- Flies taste with chemoreceptors on their feet (tarsi). General insect physiology.
- Bush fly thorax has two dark stripes; house fly (*M. domestica*) has four. CSIRO / AM fly ID guides.
- Flies (Diptera) have one pair of wings. General.
- Breed mainly in fresh cattle dung; eggs hatch into maggots that feed in the dung; dung that dries out is unsuitable. CSIRO.
- Cattle defecate roughly 10–15 times a day. General livestock references.
- Larvae pupate in soil; egg to adult in about 1–2 weeks in warm weather. CSIRO.
- CSIRO's Australian Dung Beetle Project (1965–1985) introduced dung beetles to bury cattle dung and reduce bush fly breeding. CSIRO.
- Native Australian dung beetles are mostly adapted to dry marsupial pellets, not large wet cattle pats. CSIRO.
- Western grey kangaroos graze paddocks at dawn and dusk. DBCA.
- Willie wagtails catch flies in flight and on the ground. BirdLife Australia.
- Bush flies land on cattle, sheep, kangaroos and people for the moisture and protein of skin and secretions (the "sit" of the ride). CSIRO.
- Willie wagtails follow cattle and sheep and sit on their backs, sallying after the insects the animals disturb. BirdLife Australia (Willie Wagtail profile); Museums Victoria.
- Each spring bush flies reinvade southern Australia from their inland and northern overwintering areas, displaced hundreds of kilometres on warm northerly winds. Hughes & Nicholas 1974 (J Anim Ecol 43: 411–428); CSIRO bush fly biology.

## Western grey kangaroo (*Macropus fuliginosus*)
- Kangaroos cannot move their hind legs independently on land (they can when swimming); on land they hop with both together. Australian Museum.
- Slow grazing movement is pentapedal: the tail is planted and pushes, doing as much propulsive work as the fore and hind legs together (measured in red kangaroos; the gait is shared by the large kangaroos). O'Connor, Dawson, Kram & Donelan 2014 (Biology Letters 10: 20140381).
- Western grey kangaroos graze paddocks on the fringes of Perth at dawn and dusk. DBCA.

## Australian painted lady (*Vanessa kershawi*)
- Found throughout Australia including Perth. AM; Braby, *Butterflies of Australia*.
- Eggs are small, green and laid singly on host leaves. Braby.
- Larvae feed on Asteraceae (daisy family), including everlastings and the introduced capeweed (*Arctotheca calendula*, from South Africa). Braby; DPIRD.
- Larvae tie leaves with silk into a shelter. Braby.
- Larvae moult four times (five instars); caterpillars increase in mass by hundreds of times. General Lepidoptera biology.
- Paper wasps (*Polistes*) prey on caterpillars to feed their brood. AM.
- Pupa (chrysalis) hangs from a stem; metamorphosis inside; adult emerges after about 1–2 weeks in warm weather. Braby.
- New adults pump haemolymph into their wings to expand them. General.
- Adults feed on nectar with a coiled proboscis. AM.
- Females taste host plants with chemoreceptors on their feet before laying. General Lepidoptera biology.
- Grevilleas are nectar sources but not larval hosts for this species. Braby.
- Painted ladies can travel long distances and sometimes move in large numbers. Braby.
- Kangaroo paw (*Anigozanthos manglesii*) is WA's floral emblem. WA Government.

## Termite (*Coptotermes*)
- Termites are most closely related to cockroaches (order Blattodea). CSIRO; AM.
- Colonies have a king, queen, workers and soldiers. CSIRO.
- Workers are blind and communicate by smell (pheromones) and vibration. CSIRO.
- They feed on wood; gut microbes help digest cellulose. CSIRO.
- They build with soil, chewed wood, saliva and faeces. CSIRO.
- *Coptotermes* soldiers exude a sticky white defensive fluid. CSIRO.
- Queens lay hundreds of eggs a day (mature colonies). CSIRO.
- Ants, including bull ants, raid termites; echidnas dig into mounds. AM.
- Termites recycle dead wood into soil. CSIRO.
- Winged reproductives (alates) swarm after rain to found new colonies. CSIRO.
- Red-tailed black cockatoos (forest subspecies) feed on marri and jarrah seeds. DBCA.

## Native land snail (*Bothriembryon*)
- Snails are molluscs (gastropods), not insects. AM.
- *Bothriembryon* is a native genus, most species in south-west WA. WAM.
- Snails move on a single muscular foot over mucus. AM.
- The radula carries thousands of tiny teeth. AM.
- Many land snails graze fungi, lichen, algae and decaying plant matter. WAM; general.
- Shell grows by adding material at the aperture. AM.
- In dry heat snails seal the aperture with a dried mucus epiphragm. AM.
- Eyes are at the tips of the upper tentacles. AM.
- Bobtails eat snails. WAM.
- Most land snails are hermaphrodites. AM.
- Quendas live in the bushland around Bibra Lake (Beeliar wetlands). DBCA.

## Discoveries
- Many banksias are serotinous: seeds held in cones until fire. Kings Park / WA Herbarium.
- Quendas (southern brown bandicoots) dig conical foraging holes; present in Kings Park. DBCA; Kings Park.
- Carnaby's black cockatoo feeds on banksia seeds; listed as endangered. DBCA.
- Australian magpies forage on the ground for invertebrates. BirdLife Australia.
- Echidnas open ant and termite nests with their front claws. AM.
- Bobtails (*Tiliqua rugosa*) eat snails, flowers and insects; the stumpy tail resembles the head. WAM.
- Resin bees and other solitary bees nest in pre-made holes such as bee hotels. Aussie Bee.
- Bees drink and collect water. General.
- The common garden snail (*Cornu aspersum*) and the white Italian snail (*Theba pisana*) are introduced from Europe; *Theba* climbs vegetation in summer. DPIRD.
- Silver gulls are generalist scavengers. BirdLife Australia.
- Gilgies (*Cherax quinquecarinatus*) are endemic to south-west WA. WAM.
- Diving beetles carry an air store under the elytra. AM.
- Freshwater snails graze algae. General.
- Motorbike frog (*Litoria moorei*) call resembles a motorbike changing gears. WAM.
- Most damselflies rest with wings folded along the body; dragonflies hold them flat. AM.
- The black swan is WA's bird emblem. WA Government.

## Blue-banded bee (*Amegilla*, e.g. *A. chlorocyanea* in SW WA)
- Solitary; female digs a nest burrow in soil or clay banks. AM, Aussie Bee.
- Buzz pollination via flight-muscle vibration; honey bees can't. AM.
- A bee's buzz (in flight and in buzz pollination) is made by rapid contraction of the thoracic flight muscles; a child's voice comes from the vocal folds in the larynx. AM; general physiology.
- *Dianella* has poricidal anthers and is buzz-pollinated; *Hibbertia* is buzz-pollinated. Literature on Australian buzz pollination.
- Cell stocked with pollen and nectar, egg laid, cell sealed. AM.
- Males sleep clamped to stems by their mandibles. AM.
- Kangaroo paws (*Anigozanthos*) are mainly bird-pollinated; pollen goes on the bird's head. Kings Park / WA Herbarium.
- Rainbow bee-eaters migrate south to Perth in spring; catch bees in flight and rub off the sting on a perch. BirdLife Australia.

## Peacock spider spiderling stage
- Eggs are laid in a silk sac; jumping spider mothers guard eggs in a silk retreat. AM; general salticid biology.
- Spiderlings hunt from hatching (after the first moult out of the sac). General.
- Spiderlings disperse from the mother's retreat. General.
- Springtails (Collembola) are hexapods but not insects; they jump using a folded spring (furcula). AM.
- Ants prey on small spiders; spiderling mortality is high. General.
- Spiders moult to grow (ecdysis). AM.
- Juvenile *Maratus* males are drab like females; colours appear at the final moult. Otto & Hill.

## Coastal peacock spider (*Maratus speciosus*)
- Lives in coastal dunes around Perth. WAM; Otto & Hill.
- About 5 mm long (grain-of-rice size). WAM.
- Spiders: 8 legs, 2 body parts. General.
- 8 eyes; large forward principal eyes give sharp, colour vision. AM.
- Stalks and pounces, no capture web. AM.
- Attaches a silk dragline before jumping. AM.
- Jumps partly by hydraulic (blood pressure) leg extension. Published research on salticid jumping.
- Male raises a flap with orange side fringes and waves legs in courtship. WAM; Otto & Hill.
- The courtship display combines a raised, tipped opisthosomal fan, waving of the ornamented third legs and substrate-borne vibrations (drumming), in a structured multi-modal sequence. Girard, Kasumovic & Elias 2011 (PLOS ONE, described for *M. volans*); the leg-III stance for *M. speciosus*: Otto & Hill 2011 (Peckhamia).
- Whether a female stays depends on display quality; unreceptive females walk away. Girard, Elias & Kasumovic 2015 (Proc R Soc B).
- The fan's blue-green colours come from structurally coloured scales (multilayer and grating reflectors), the red-orange from pigment. Stavenga, Otto & Wilts 2016 (J R Soc Interface); Hsiung et al. 2017 (Nature Communications).
- Spider wasps (Pompilidae) paralyse spiders and lay an egg on them. AM.
- Almost all *Maratus* species are Australian; new species still being described. Otto & Hill.

## Blue skimmer nymph stage
- Dragonflies start life as aquatic nymphs (larvae). AM.
- Nymphs are ambush predators. AM.
- Wing pads develop on the back; no functional wings until the final moult. General odonate biology.
- Labium: hinged, hooked lower lip that shoots out to seize prey in a fraction of a second. AM; Corbet, *Dragonflies: Behaviour and Ecology of Odonata*.
- Dragonfly (Anisoptera) nymphs breathe through gills inside the rectum and can jet forward by expelling water. Corbet.
- Larger nymphs take tadpoles and small fish. Corbet.
- Nymphs moult many times (about 9–17 instars across species). Corbet.
- Mosquito larvae ("wrigglers") hang at the surface and breathe through a siphon. CSIRO / health department mosquito guides.
- Oblong turtle (*Chelodina oblonga*, also listed as *C. colliei*) is endemic to south-west WA wetlands, including Herdsman Lake; eats aquatic invertebrates, tadpoles and fish. WAM; DBCA.
- Final-instar nymph climbs a stem, the skin splits, and the adult emerges; the cast skin is an exuvia. AM.

## Blue skimmer (*Orthetrum caledonicum*)
- Common in Perth wetlands. ALA records.
- Males powder-blue, females yellow-brown. AM.
- Catch prey in flight with spiny legs. General odonate biology.
- Large compound eyes, near-360° vision. General.
- Each of 4 wings moves independently. General.
- Some dragonflies catch over 90% of prey they chase. Combes et al. 2012 (J Exp Biol).
- Nymphs live underwater for months; extendable labium. AM.
- Nymphs eat mosquito larvae. General.
- Female libellulids such as *Orthetrum* lay eggs by dipping the abdomen tip into water in flight. Corbet.
- Australian hobby (*Falco longipennis*) takes dragonflies in flight. BirdLife Australia.

## Coastal sandhopper (family Talitridae)
- Talitrid amphipods (sandhoppers, beach fleas) live on sandy beaches around Australia, including Perth's coast. AM; WAM.
- They are crustaceans (Amphipoda), with many pairs of legs, not insects. AM.
- They shelter in burrows in damp sand by day and come out at dusk and night to feed. AM; Griffiths & Stenton-Dozey (beach ecology).
- They eat stranded seaweed (wrack) and help break it down. AM; Dugan et al. on wrack and beach food webs.
- They jump by flicking the abdomen and tail appendages (uropods) against the sand. AM.
- Female amphipods carry eggs in a brood pouch (marsupium) under the body; young hatch in the pouch and leave as tiny juveniles. AM.
- They breathe with gills and must stay moist. AM.
- Talitrids orient using the sun and moon (sun and moon compass). Pardi & Papi; Ugolini et al.
- Silver gulls (*Chroicocephalus novaehollandiae*) and red-capped plovers (*Charadrius ruficapillus*) forage for small invertebrates on Perth beaches. BirdLife Australia.
- Port Beach is in North Fremantle, just north of Fremantle Harbour, whose container cranes are visible from the beach. City of Fremantle.

## Southern house mosquito (*Culex quinquefasciatus*)
- Common in Perth suburbs; breeds in still water in containers, drains and ponds. WA Department of Health.
- Only females bite; they need a blood meal to develop eggs. Males feed on nectar. WA Dept of Health; AM.
- Mosquitoes find hosts using carbon dioxide in breath, body odour and heat. AM; WA Dept of Health.
- The mouthparts (proboscis) are a bundle of fine stylets; saliva contains anticoagulants, and the reaction to saliva causes itching. AM.
- *Culex* females lay eggs stuck together in floating rafts on still water. WA Dept of Health; Medical Entomology (Westmead).
- Larvae (wrigglers) develop in still water; tipping out containers weekly is the standard advice. WA Dept of Health "Fight the Bite".
- Mainly active at dusk and night. WA Dept of Health.
- A mosquito can take in more than its own body weight in blood. AM.
- People's defensive movements (waving, slapping) drive mosquitoes off and interrupt their feeding, so a mosquito often has to try again. Edman & Kale 1971, *Annals of the Entomological Society of America* 64(2): 513–516; "Host defensive behaviour and the feeding success of mosquitoes", *Insect Science and its Application* 1987. The game shows only the waving.
- Marbled geckos (*Christinus marmoratus*) live in southwest WA and hunt insects at night, often near lights. WAM.

## Painted lady flight stage
- Painted ladies are strong fliers; *Vanessa* butterflies make long movements across Australia. AM; Braby, *Butterflies of Australia*.
- Butterflies warm their flight muscles in the sun before flying. AM.
- Butterflies see ultraviolet light and UV patterns on flowers. General insect vision (Briscoe & Chittka).
- Butterfly flight uses unsteady aerodynamics (clap-and-fling and figure-of-eight wing paths) to create lift. Srygley & Thomas (Nature 2002).
- Butterflies alternate flapping and gliding. Braby.
- The proboscis uncoils to sip nectar. AM.
- Butterflies carry pollen between flowers. AM.
- Willie wagtails catch insects in flight. BirdLife Australia.
- Added in 3.20. The "Fremantle Doctor" is Perth's afternoon sea breeze, blowing in off the Indian Ocean (Bureau of Meteorology blog; ABC, which puts sea breezes on nearly 60% of days). In the level it arrives after the second flower, up above the trees only, from the south-west.
- Scenery (3.20): Bold Park is mostly banksia and tuart woodland, with kangaroo paws among its spring colour (BGPA, Bold Park Bushland; the species of kangaroo paw is not given there, so they are drawn as plain red-stemmed ones). From Reabold Hill, 85 m, the Indian Ocean, Perth city and Rottnest can all be seen (BGPA), so the sea with its surf, the city and a low Rottnest on the horizon are in the view. Pretend: the hunter losing a butterfly that flies low among the plants, the rings over the flowers, and the wind's exact strength and height.

## Blue skimmer, adult female
- Female *O. caledonicum* are yellow-brown; males develop blue pruinescence. Theischinger & Hawking, *The Complete Field Guide to Dragonflies of Australia*.
- Females lay eggs by tapping the abdomen tip on the water in flight; the male often guards nearby (non-contact guarding) to keep rival males away. Corbet, *Dragonflies: Behavior and Ecology of Odonata*.
- Eggs hatch into aquatic nymphs. Corbet.

## Peacock spider, adult female
- Female *Maratus* are drab brown and cryptic. Otto & Hill (Peckhamia).
- Males court with a raised, coloured fan, leg waving and substrate-borne vibrations (drumming). Girard, Kasumovic & Elias (2011).
- Females choose among courting males; female choice drives the evolution of male colour and dance. Girard et al.
- Females prefer males whose display is complete and vigorous (visual and vibratory components together). Girard, Elias & Kasumovic 2015 (Proc R Soc B).
- Fan colours arise from structurally coloured scales. Stavenga, Otto & Wilts 2016; Hsiung et al. 2017.
- Female jumping spiders lay eggs in a silk sac inside a retreat and guard them. AM; Jackson (salticid biology).
- Ants are predators of spider eggs and spiderlings. General.
- Females are choosy: in a lab study of *M. volans*, 16 of 64 courtships of virgin females ended in mating (about 1 in 4). Girard, Elias & Kasumovic 2015, "Female preference for multi-modal courtship: multiple signals are important for male mating success in peacock spiders", Proc R Soc B 282: 20152222.
- The courtship parts the suitors show (raised fan, waving third legs, drumming, and the fan with the leg wave together) are the ones described for *M. volans*. Girard, Kasumovic & Elias 2011, PLOS ONE 6: e25390, with its supplementary videos.
- Mated females almost never mate again: in Girard's *M. volans* trials none of the mated females re-mated, and mated females were more aggressive to later suitors and paid them less attention. Girard & Endler 2014, "Peacock spiders", Current Biology 24: R588–R590 (unpublished data reported there); Girard in The Conversation, December 2015. The game's second mate is therefore labelled pretend.
- About 5 to 13 eggs in a silken sac attached to leaves or twigs; about two weeks to develop, then the young stay in the sac about two more weeks, moult there under the mother's guard and leave; the mother goes about four weeks without food. Jürgen Otto's notes on an undescribed *Maratus* species (his Flickr album "Maratus sp. 4, development"); Otto & Hill, Peckhamia, on second-instar *M. personatus* spiderlings leaving the nest.
- Spiderlings hunt from hatching; each is already a small jumping spider. See the spiderling stage above.

## Bug family tree
- Molluscs and arthropods are separate animal phyla. General zoology.
- Arthropods have an exoskeleton and jointed legs and moult to grow. AM.
- Chelicerates (spiders, scorpions, mites) have chelicerae and 8 legs. AM.
- Insects (hexapods) are nested within Pancrustacea: crustaceans and insects form one group. Regier et al. (Nature 2010); Misof et al. (Science 2014).
- Odonata are an early-branching winged-insect lineage; griffinflies (Meganisoptera) flew about 300 million years ago. Misof et al.; fossil record.
- Termites are social cockroaches (Blattodea). Inward, Beccaloni & Eggleton (Biology Letters 2007).
- Holometabola (complete metamorphosis) include Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera and Diptera. Misof et al.
- Diptera have halteres (reduced hind wings) for balance. AM.

## Who you are (sex tags)
- Bull ant workers are female; queens are female. AM.
- Coptotermes workers include both sexes. Termite biology (Eggleton).
- *Bothriembryon* and other land snails are hermaphrodites. AM; WAM.
- Juvenile spiderlings and odonate nymphs can't be sexed by eye. General.

## Motorbike frog (*Litoria moorei*), the frog chorus level
- The motorbike frog (western bell frog) lives in wetlands and lakes around Perth; the male's call is a series of rising notes like a motorbike changing gears, followed by warbling growls; calls spring to summer. WAM Frog Watch; Frogwatch WA.
- Western banjo frog (*Limnodynastes dorsalis*): a single "bonk" like a plucked banjo string; a chorus sounds like a band of bad banjo players; calls mostly winter and spring. WAM Frog Watch; Frogwatch WA.
- Moaning frog (*Heleioporus eyrei*): a long, low, rising moan, called from a burrow in sandy soil; mainly late autumn and early winter. WAM Frog Watch; Frogwatch WA.
- Squelching froglet (*Crinia insignifera*): a short rubbery squelch; a small frog under 3 cm; calls through winter and spring. WAM Frog Watch; Frogwatch WA.
- All four species are recorded around Lake Joondalup (Yellagonga Regional Park) and other Perth wetlands. ALA; DBCA (Yellagonga).
- Male frogs call to attract females, who choose a mate by his call; a calling male inflates the vocal sac, which makes the call louder. Gerhardt & Huber 2002, *Acoustic Communication in Insects and Anurans*; Wells 2007, *The Ecology and Behavior of Amphibians*.
- Frogs in a chorus avoid overlapping their calls, alternating with their neighbours so each can be heard. Wells 1977 (Animal Behaviour); Gerhardt & Huber 2002.
- Frogs are amphibians, vertebrates with a backbone, and start life as tadpoles that breathe with gills; adults eat insects and other invertebrates. AM; WAM.
- The nankeen night heron hunts at night and dusk along the water's edge for fish, frogs, crustaceans and insects, and is common at Perth lakes. BirdLife Australia; ALA.
- Oblong turtles eat tadpoles among other prey (see the dragonfly nymph section). WAM.
- Frogs are indicators of wetland health. Frogwatch WA; WAM.
- Lake Joondalup, in Yellagonga Regional Park, is one of Perth's largest freshwater wetlands. DBCA.

## Bug Olympics (six events at Perry Lakes)
The events are invented games; each is a thing the bug really does, and every number in the text is sourced here. The game says so on every event page ("The games are pretend, but the numbers are real").
- Perry Lakes Stadium (Floreat) was built for, and held the athletics of, the 1962 British Empire and Commonwealth Games (22 November to 1 December 1962). Commonwealth Games Federation results; Wikipedia (Athletics at the 1962 British Empire and Commonwealth Games).
- Long jump. Sandhoppers jump by flicking the abdomen and tail appendages (uropods) against the sand, not with their legs (see the sandhopper section). AM. The jump is a body catapult: muscle alone cannot supply the measured power, so energy is stored and released like a spring; take-off velocities of 0.4 to 1.9 m/s in *Talitrus saltator*. "Body-catapult mechanism of the sandhopper jump and its biomimetic implications", *Acta Biomaterialia* 2021 (doi 10.1016/j.actbio.2021.01.033). No peer-reviewed figure for the distance in body lengths was found, so the game says only "many times its own length" (AM). Jumps in body lengths as the usual way to compare jumpers of different sizes: Burrows 2003 (Nature 424: 509, froghoppers). The men's long jump world record is 8.95 m (Mike Powell, 1991), about five times a 1.8 m athlete's height. World Athletics.
- The "kid" line uses a child about 1.2 m tall (the median height at 6 to 7 years is about 1.15 to 1.25 m; WHO growth reference), a 25 m swimming pool, a 12 m bus and a 4.5 m car; the metres shown are the player's own body-length count times 1.2.
- Sprint. "Some of the larger dragonflies can reach flying speeds of 70 km per hour." Australian Museum (Dragonflies and damselflies, Order Odonata). The fastest reliably timed insect flight is the Australian dragonfly *Austrophlebia costalis* at 58 km/h in short bursts (39 km/h sustained). Guinness World Records (Fastest flying insect). The comparison "as fast as a car on a main road": Western Australia's default built-up-area limit is 50 km/h and many main roads are 60 to 70 km/h. WA Road Safety Commission. Each wing moves independently (see the blue skimmer section).
- Dung burying. Tunnelling dung beetles dig beneath the pat and pack the tunnels with dung as brood balls; an egg is laid in each and the larva feeds on it; tunnels can reach about a metre deep. CSIRO (Dung beetles; Dung beetle program). CSIRO's Australian Dung Beetle Project (1965 to 1985) introduced dung beetles to bury cattle dung and cut bush fly breeding (see the bush fly section). Both sexes take part in nesting in tunnelling species (the female digs and provisions, the male brings dung). Bornemissza 1976 (CSIRO Division of Entomology; the Australian Dung Beetle Project); Halffter & Edmonds 1982, *The Nesting Behavior of Dung Beetles*.
- Carry relay. Ants carry loads several times their own body mass; measured leafcutter ants (*Atta colombica*) carried loads up to about 8 times their own mass. Lighton, Bartholomew & Feener 1987 (Physiological Zoology 60: 524–537). Large prey is cut up or carried cooperatively by groups of ants. Czaczkes & Ratnieks 2013 (Myrmecological News 18: 1–11). The game gives no multiplier for bull ants ("many times heavier than themselves", AM, as in the ant section). "A kid carrying a grown-up": several times a 25 kg child is an adult's weight.
- Buzz-off. The blue-banded bee *Amegilla murrayensis* buzz-pollinates by banging its head against the anthers about 350 times a second (buzzes at about 350 Hz, against about 240 Hz for the bumblebee *Bombus impatiens*), and spends less time on each flower, usually buzzing it once; tomatoes need buzz pollination. Switzer, Hogendoorn, Ravi & Combes 2016, "Shakers and head bangers: differences in sonication behavior between Australian *Amegilla murrayensis* (blue-banded bees) and North American *Bombus impatiens* (bumblebees)", Arthropod-Plant Interactions 10: 1–8. A bee's buzz comes from the flight muscles (see the bee section).
- Gymnastics. Click beetles (Elateridae) right themselves without their legs: a peg on the prosternum is locked in a notch on the mesosternum, the muscles load it, and when it slips the jolt throws the beetle into the air; the launch is near vertical (the beetle cannot aim it), the beetle somersaults in the air, and it lands on its feet no more often than chance, so it may click several times. Evans 1972 (J Zool 167: 319–336) and 1973 (J Zool 169: 181–194); Ribak & Weihs 2011, "Jumping without using legs: the jump of the click-beetles (Elateridae) is morphologically constrained", PLoS ONE 6: e20871. Evans measured jumps of about 30 cm (more than 25 body lengths), up to six somersaults, and a peak acceleration of about 380 g; the game's full-power flip is set to about 25 body lengths and six somersaults (never more than six are counted). The Land press counts in the last 0.45 s before touchdown. The 3 g comparison: the Space Shuttle's ascent was limited to 3 g (NASA). Click beetles live in Perth: *Macromalocera coenosa* has the Swan River as its type locality (Australian Faunal Directory); Australian Museum (Click beetles). Points, the power bar and the Land button are the game's own: a real beetle cannot choose its height, its somersaults or its landing, and the event page says so. The "kid" line uses a child about 1.2 m tall and a storey of about 3 m (a house roof about 6 m).
- Medals are the game's own invention (3, 2 and 1 stars); the thresholds are tuned so the test bots get gold (perfect) and silver or bronze (kid).

## Bug Circus (five acts at the Big Top)
The acts are invented tricks; each is a thing the bug really does, and every number and behaviour in the text is sourced here. Every act page says "The circus is pretend".
- Landmarks: Cirque du Soleil's big top has played Perth at Belmont Park Racecourse (KOOZA, 2017) and, since 2020, at Claremont Showground (KURIOS 2020, LUZIA), which the game uses; a touring big top (THE CIRCUS) stood at Langley Park in June and July 2022. Claremont Showground; Cirque du Soleil.
- The Strongbug. Large male horned dung beetles (*Onthophagus taurus*) fight horn to horn inside the tunnels that lead to females and shove the rival out; longer horns win more fights, while small hornless males sneak past the guards instead. Moczek & Emlen 2000, "Male horn dimorphism in the scarab beetle, Onthophagus taurus: do alternative reproductive tactics favour alternative phenotypes?", Animal Behaviour 59: 459–466. In pull tests with weighted threads, males pulled up to 1,141 times their own body weight, the greatest relative strength measured for an insect. Knell & Simmons 2010, "Mating tactics determine patterns of condition dependence in a dimorphic horned beetle", Proc R Soc B 277 (the 1,141× figure). The tunnel across the ring, the three rivals, the taps, the wind-up heaves and the crowd rally are the game's own. *O. taurus* was introduced to Western Australia in the early 1970s for dung burial and is now the dominant exotic dung beetle across the south-west. Moczek, Hunt, Emlen & Simmons 2002 (Evolutionary Ecology Research); Dung Beetle Ecosystem Engineers species notes. "Twenty cars": a 25 kg child times 1,141 is about 28 tonnes, about twenty 1.4-tonne cars.
- The flying trapeze. Jumping spiders (Salticidae) attach a silk dragline before a jump; high-speed video shows it is more than a safety line: tension on it corrects the body's pitch in flight so the spider lands on its feet, and spiders without it slip or tumble on landing; a spider that misses hangs on the line and climbs back. Chen, Liao, Tsai & Chi 2013, "More than a safety line: jump-stabilizing silk of salticids", J R Soc Interface 10: 20130572. Peacock spiders are salticids with big front eyes for judging distance (see the coastal peacock spider section). The swinging bars and the catches are the game's own.
- The tightrope. Orb-weavers start a web by releasing a silk line that the wind carries until it snags on something on the far side (bridging), then pull it taut and cross it. Eberhard 1987, "How spiders initiate airborne lines", J Arachnol 15: 1–9; Gregorič, Agnarsson, Blackledge & Kuntner 2011, "How did the spider cross the river? Behavioral adaptations for river-bridging webs in *Caerostris darwini*", PLoS ONE. Spiders grip silk with tarsal claws and serrated bristles. Foelix 2011, *Biology of Spiders*. Spider dragline silk stretches by about a third of its length before it breaks. Gosline, Guerette, Ortlepp & Savage 1999, J Exp Biol 202: 3295–3303. Golden orb-weaver silk: see the golden orb-weaver section. The posts, the gusts and the Hold on button are the game's own.
- The disappearing act. Bark-resting moths walk to a spot and turn their body to match the bark's furrows, which measurably improves their camouflage against predators that hunt by sight. Kang, Moon, Lee & Jablonski 2012, "Camouflage through an active choice of a resting spot and body orientation in moths", J Evol Biol 25: 1695–1702; Kang et al. 2013 (Proc R Soc B) showed the moths read the bark's structure to choose the orientation. Movement breaks camouflage. Stevens & Merilaita 2009, "Animal camouflage: current issues and new perspectives", Phil Trans R Soc B 364: 423–427. New Holland honeyeaters take insects as well as nectar and forage by sight. BirdLife Australia. The game's moth is the brown noctuid of the web level; the bark patches, the spotlight, the rounds and the Settle button are the game's own.
- Walking on water. Water striders (Gerridae) stand on the water's surface tension on legs covered in microscopic water-repellent hairs (Gao & Jiang 2004, "Water-repellent legs of water striders", Nature 432: 36) and row with the middle legs, which press dimples into the surface and shed vortices, gliding between strokes (Hu, Chan & Bush 2003, "The hydrodynamics of water strider locomotion", Nature 424: 663–666). They feed on insects that fall onto the water, found by the ripples they make. Andersen 1982, *The Semiaquatic Bugs*. Gerridae live across Western Australia on still and slow water (for example *Limnogonus fossarum gilguy* and *Tenagogerris* species). Andersen & Weir 1997, "The gerrid bugs (Hemiptera: Gerridae) of Australia", Invertebrate Taxonomy 11: 203–299. The game's strider is a generic gerrid, not a named species; the pool and the Row button are the game's own.
- Cheers are the game's own invention (3, 2 and 1 stars), tuned so the test bots get three cheers (perfect) and one or two (kid).

## Golden orb-weaver (*Trichonephila edulis*)
- Found across Australia including Perth; webs of yellow-gold silk; females large, males tiny and often on the web edge. AM; WAM.
- Orb webs: frame and radial (dry) threads plus a sticky capture spiral with glue droplets. Foelix, *Biology of Spiders*.
- Silk is made in glands and drawn from spinnerets. Foelix.
- Prey is wrapped in silk then bitten. AM.
- Spiders digest externally: they pour digestive fluid onto prey and suck up the liquid. Foelix.
- Orb-weavers often cut unwanted prey (e.g. stinging bees and wasps) out of the web. Foelix; AM.
- Webs are repaired often; golden orb-weaver webs can persist for long periods. AM.
- Dewdrop spiders (*Argyrodes*) live in orb webs and steal small prey (kleptoparasites). AM.
- Spider silk is among the strongest natural materials for its weight. General.
- Christmas beetles (*Anoplognathus*) are heavy fliers. AM.

## Sugar ant (*Camponotus*)
- *Camponotus* sugar ants are common in Perth; mostly active at dusk and night. AM; WAM.

- Nests have chambers for brood and food; ants keep rubbish in middens. Hölldobler & Wilson; Tschinkel (nest architecture).

## London (the second map: real London animals)
Found by tapping the plane at Perth Airport on the Perth map; the plane lands on a London map with a pin for each level, and the bus tour plays them all. Side-on levels. Every animal and plant in them lives in London.
- The London map is a storybook map, not to scale: the Thames, Regent's Park, Hampstead Heath, Highgate Wood, Alexandra Palace and its mast, Richmond Park, Greenwich, Epping Forest, the BT Tower, St Paul's, Tower Bridge, the Shard, the London Eye, Wembley's arch, Heathrow. Scenery only. London's buses have been red for well over a hundred years: the London General Omnibus Company took up the red of its rival Vanguard in 1907–08 to stand out from other operators, and red has been the colour ever since (London Transport Museum, "Why are London buses red?"). No fact in the game states the year.
- **The plane**: Perth to London non-stop since 25 March 2018 (Qantas QF9, Perth to Heathrow): 14,498 km in about 17 hours. Qantas; press coverage of the first flight (BBC, ABC, 2018).
- **Buff-tailed bumblebee** (*Bombus terrestris*), Regent's Park: one of the commonest bumblebees in the UK's parks and gardens; workers are female, 11–17 mm; she carries nectar in her honey stomach as fuel and packs pollen into the pollen baskets on her hind legs; colonies often nest underground in old mouse and vole holes. Bumblebee Conservation Trust (species guide; "bumblebee lifecycle"). In London some colonies stay active all winter, feeding on garden shrubs such as Mahonia: Stelzer et al. 2010, *PLoS ONE* 5(3): e9559.
- Buff-tailed bumblebees have short tongues and are keen nectar robbers: they bite a hole in the side of a deep flower such as comfrey and drink without going in (BBC Wildlife / Discover Wildlife, "What is nectar robbing?"; Natural History Society of Northumbria, "Nectar robbing"). Bees leave scent on flowers they visit (footprint cues) and other bees avoid recently visited flowers, which are likely to be empty; the effect fades as the nectar comes back, after about 40 minutes (Stout, Goulson & Allen 1998; Stout & Goulson 2001, *Animal Behaviour*; Pearce, Giuggioli & Rands 2017, *Scientific Reports*). In the game an emptied flower stays empty. Bumblebees caught in heavy rain shelter under flowers and leaves (Bumblebee Conservation Trust, bad weather FAQ and photographs). Dogs that snap at bees and wasps are often stung on the face or in the mouth (Vets Now; Joii Pet Care). London Zoo is in Regent's Park; its Giraffe House (Decimus Burton, 1836–37) is still used for giraffes (ZSL; Historic England listing 1357403). The BT Tower is visible from the park. The rival bee, the sunhat and buzzing the giraffe are the game's own.
- Flower crab spider (*Misumena vatia*): sits still on a flower and ambushes bees and flies that land there. British Arachnological Society (species page); The Wildlife Trusts.
- Feral pigeons across London (RSPB, feral pigeon); grey squirrels, brought from eastern North America in Victorian times and released in Regent's Park and Richmond Park among other places (Horniman Museum, "Where did London's domestic wildlife come from?").
- **Common wasp** (*Vespula vulgaris*), a café garden: workers (all female, 12–17 mm) chew wood into pulp and build the paper nest; they hunt insects such as caterpillars and flies to feed the grubs, and drink the sugary saliva the grubs give back; by late summer a nest holds thousands of wasps (5,000–10,000). Buglife (common wasp); Big Wasp Survey ("All about wasps"). Late in summer the nest stops raising grubs, so workers look for sugar elsewhere, including cafés and picnics: BBC Future on the Big Wasp Survey (Sumner, UCL).
- Social wasps chew their prey into a paste or a packet of meat to carry home to the larvae (NC State Extension, "Social wasps"; *Psyche* 103: 85, on the yellowjacket routine). People set jam-jar traps for wasps: jam and water in a jar, a paper lid with a hole (RHS, "Wasps (social) including hornets", which doubts they help). Garden spiders' webs catch wasps (The Wildlife Trusts, garden spider). Late-summer workers seek sugar (see above), hence the jam and the lemonade. The customers' red zones, the wave that blows her off and the bus's top deck over the wall are the game's own.
- London plane trees line many London streets (Woodland Trust, London plane; London Museum, "What's a London plane tree?"). London's house sparrows fell by about 71% from 1995 (Dadam et al. 2019, *Scientific Reports*, ZSL/RSPB/BTO).
- **Underground mosquito** (*Culex pipiens* form *molestus*): lives and breeds in the London Underground, bites people, rats and mice, and breeds all year in the warm tunnels; females can lay their first batch of eggs without a blood meal (autogeny). Byrne & Nichols 1999, *Heredity* 82: 7–15. It did not evolve in the Tube: its ancestors came from the Mediterranean or Middle East over 1,000 years ago (Haba et al. 2025, *Science*; Natural History Museum news). *Culex* females lay their eggs together in a raft that floats on the water (University of Florida IFAS, *Culex* fact sheet; CDC mosquito life cycle).
- The Tube level's hosts follow Byrne & Nichols 1999: this mosquito feeds on rats, mice and people. Her first raft laid without blood is its autogeny (same paper). The commuters' red zones (and that someone on a phone never looks up), the Perth poster, "Mind the gap" and the web are the game's own; spiders do catch mosquitoes.
- Moving trains push air through the tunnels (the piston effect), so a wind blows along the platform as a train comes (Cross et al., University of Sheffield, tunnel aerodynamics; London Underground ventilation). The world's first underground railway, the Metropolitan Railway, opened in London on 10 January 1863 (Science Museum Group; Guinness World Records). Around half a million mice live in the Underground, living mostly on dropped food (TfL FOI reply, 2019; Time Out).
- **Jersey tiger moth** (*Euplagia quadripunctaria*), a garden in south-east London: colonised south-east London around 2004–05 and is spreading (Butterfly Conservation; The Guardian, Specieswatch); stripy black-and-cream forewings over bright orange (sometimes red or yellow) hindwings; flies by day and by night, July to September; adults sip nectar from garden flowers; eggs are laid on the larval food plants and caterpillars eat nettles, bramble and ground ivy; they overwinter as small caterpillars (The Wildlife Trusts, Jersey tiger; UKMoths; Wildlife Insight). Wingspan about 52–65 mm, forewing 23–33 mm. No source confirms that it flashes its underwings to startle birds, so the game doesn't use a flash.
- Jersey tigers are often seen nectaring on buddleia (Butterfly Conservation, Jersey tiger; The Wildlife Trusts). Flying insects turn their backs to a nearby light, which leaves them circling it (Fabian et al. 2024, *Nature Communications* 15: 689, "Why flying insects gather at artificial light"); the pull and the "Bonk!" are the game's version of it. The cat batting at moths, the washing line and the sleeping dad are the game's own.
- Great tits hunt caterpillars and moths, and feed their chicks mostly on moth caterpillars (RSPB, great tit; University of Tartu nestling diet studies). Song thrushes smash snails on a stone "anvil" (RSPB, song thrush). Blackbirds are among the first birds to sing at dawn (RSPB, dawn chorus).
- **Stag beetle** (*Lucanus cervus*), Richmond Park at dusk: Britain's biggest land beetle; males 35–75 mm with their jaws, females 30–50 mm (Buglife; NHM). London and the Thames Valley are its UK stronghold (NHM "Stag beetles"; London Wildlife Trust). Only males have the antler-like jaws, used to wrestle rival males. The grubs live in rotting wood underground for at least three to four years; adults live only a few weeks and can't eat solid food, living on fat stored as grubs; males fly at dusk on warm evenings in May to August, slowly and clumsily, looking for females; foxes, cats, magpies, crows and kestrels eat them (PTES, stag beetle factsheet and Great Stag Hunt). Females lay eggs by buried dead wood (PTES).
- Male stag beetles wrestle on logs, each trying to get his jaws under the other and tip him off (NHM, "Stag beetles"; PTES). Adults can't eat solid food but drink from sap runs and soft fallen fruit with a feathery tongue (PTES, "7 things you never knew about stag beetles"; PTES fact file). Many are trodden on or run over on paths and roads (PTES). The pond, the joggers and the push meter are the game's own.
- Richmond Park has about 1,300 veteran oak trees (Friends of Richmond Park; Royal Parks) and about 600 red and fallow deer (Royal Parks); male deer cast their antlers each spring and grow new ones (British Deer Society; Mammal Society). Foxes are common in London's parks and gardens (Mammal Society; London Wildlife Trust).
- **The Ally Pally wasp** (a queen common wasp, *Vespula vulgaris*), Alexandra Palace in winter: the PDC World Darts Championship is played at Alexandra Palace each December and January, and a wasp has turned up on stage in many of them. Sky Sports traces it to Adrian Lewis being stung in his first-round match in December 2011; in 2017 one landed on a player during Suljović v Painter; in December 2023 Ross Smith was stung on stage (Sky Sports, "Alexandra Palace wasp stars at 2026 World Darts Championship, but why does the phenomenon exist?"; Sky Sports, 18 December 2017; PA via BreakingNews.ie, 20 December 2023). The game says it "has landed on players, and even stung them". Prof. Seirian Sumner (UCL) told BBC Sport it is most likely a queen (the workers are dead by December) and that indoors they "are drawn to light and heat, therefore end up at the stage" (BBC Sport, "The Ally Pally wasps are back, but where are they coming from?"; UCL News, January 2024).
- In social wasps the workers and males die off in late autumn; only the newly mated queens hibernate through winter, in places such as lofts, sheds, tree hollows and under bark, and each starts a nest in spring (NHM, "What do wasps do?"; BWARS, Vespinae; Woodland Trust, "How big is a queen wasp?"). A few colonies now and then last into winter (BWARS), so the game says the workers "die in autumn" as the rule. A hibernating queen tucks her antennae between her legs and holds on by her jaws (Manaaki Whenua Landcare Research, "Life cycle of a wasp"); the same page says a new queen is nearly 40% fat by dry weight and lives on that store through winter. That waking early uses the store up sooner, with no food about, is the usual explanation for queens lost in mild winters (BPCA, "PestWatch: queen wasps"; for paper wasps, measured: PMC10672273); the game says only that awake "she uses it up faster". *Vespula* wasps make their own heat with their flight muscles and need a warm thorax to take off (Kovac et al. 2015, *J Comp Physiol A*, doi 10.1007/s00359-015-1033-4); the game calls it "a bit like shivering". A queen is about 20 mm long, against 12–17 mm for a worker (Woodland Trust, Nature's Calendar, "Wasp (queen)").
- Three darts in the treble 20 score 180, the most possible with three darts (PDC Europe, rules). Alexandra Palace opened on 24 May 1873 and burnt down sixteen days later; the second palace opened on 1 May 1875 (Alexandra Palace, "1875: the second palace"; Historic England). On 2 November 1936 the BBC began "the world's first high-definition television service" from its studios there (Alexandra Palace, "BBC at the Palace"; Historic England listing 1268256); the game says "the world's first proper TV service". The players, the caller, the scores, the trophy, the stage smoke, the foam hands and the spilled drink are the game's own; nobody in it is a real person.
- **Pill woodlouse** (*Armadillidium vulgare*), Highgate Wood: rolls into a ball when disturbed; of British woodlice only the pill woodlice (*Armadillidium*) and one other can (The Wildlife Trusts, pill woodlouse; British Myriapod and Isopod Group, *A. vulgare*). Up to 18 mm long (BMIG; The Wildlife Trusts give 11–18 mm). Woodlice are crustaceans, closer to crabs and shrimps than to insects, with seven pairs of legs (The Wildlife Trusts, common woodlouse); they breathe with gill-like pleopods under the rear of the body and must stay moist (BMIG, "An introduction to woodlice"). A rolled-up pill woodlouse loses water about a third more slowly (Smigel & Gibbs 2008, *J Insect Science* 8: 44). They eat dead wood, leaf litter and fungi, and their own droppings; the usual explanation for the last is recycling copper, which their blood is based on (Woodland Trust, common rough woodlouse and pill woodlouse; Discover Wildlife, "Facts about the common rough woodlouse"; The Wildlife Trusts, "an important nutrient-recycler"). Woodlice can take in water through the anus as well as the mouth (Spencer & Edney 1954, *J Exp Biol* 31: 491–496, "The absorption of water by woodlice"). They moult in two halves, the back half two or three days before the front, and sometimes eat the cast skin (BMIG, same guide; Slater Museum of Natural History, common woodlouse). The mother carries eggs and newly hatched young (mancas) in a fluid-filled brood pouch under her body (BMIG; RHS, woodlice).
- Ants prey on woodlice, and rolling up is one of the pill woodlouse's defences (Tuf & Ďurajková 2022, *ZooKeys* 1101, "Antipredatory strategies of terrestrial isopods"); no source says ants cannot grip a ball, and the game doesn't say so. Centipedes, spiders, shrews and toads eat woodlice (RSPB, Flatford blog, "Focus on woodlice"). The woodlouse spider (*Dysdera crocata*) hides by day in a silk cell under stones and logs and hunts at night without a web, with very large fangs; the British Arachnological Society says there is no evidence the fangs pierce the armour (it takes woodlice from below), so the game says only "huge fangs for catching woodlice".
- Highgate Wood is 28 hectares of ancient woodland, mostly hornbeam with oak standards, kept by the City of London Corporation since 1886; wild service tree grows there and is an indicator of ancient woodland (City of London, Highgate Wood management plan 2025–35). Roman potters worked in the wood between about AD 50 and 160: digs in the 1960s and 70s found at least ten kilns and over 40,000 sherds (City of London, "Roman kilns in Highgate Wood"; Brown & Sheldon 2018, excavations 1966–78). The lost thing there is a pot shard.
- **Red fox** (*Vulpes vulpes*), a south London street on a spring night: foxes have been recorded in southern British towns since the 1930s, when suburbs of houses with gardens were built (NHM, "The secret life of urban foxes"; The Fox Website, University of Bristol, urban foxes FAQ); London has about 10,000, an estimate (London Wildlife Trust, foxes policy). They are active mostly from dusk to dawn (Woodland Trust, fox; an Oxford radio-tracking study of urban foxes). In London about 21% of what foxes eat is invertebrates, chiefly earthworms, beetles and craneflies (The Fox Website, urban diet, from Harris 1981, *Mammal Review* 11: 151–168; RSPCA, foxes); town foxes often catch pigeons and rats (Woodland Trust, "What do foxes eat?").
- Foxes hunt earthworms on lawns on mild, damp nights, finding them mainly by ear, and grip a worm in the front teeth and ease it out rather than tugging, which would snap it (Macdonald 1980, *Z. Tierpsychol.* 52: 171–200, as summarised by Wildlife Online; GWCT, species of the month, October 2010). A fox finds a mouse by the sounds it makes, leaps into the air and comes down on it from above (The Fox Website, communication; National Geographic, "Foxes use the Earth's magnetic field as a targeting system"). Adults bring shoes, balls and dog chews back to the earth as toys for the cubs (The Fox Website, growing up; RSPCA, foxes). Droppings are left in obvious places, on objects, paths and even food remains, and urine marks the borders of the territory (The Fox Website; The Wildlife Trusts, red fox). Earths are commonly under garden sheds; most cubs are born in March in litters of four or five and come above ground in April; both parents bring them food (NHM, same article; Mammal Society, red fox; The Fox Website). When a fox meets a cat it is usually the fox that backs off (Cats Protection, "Are foxes a danger to cats?", citing a Royal Veterinary College study). London's N-prefix buses run at night only, from about midnight until early morning (TfL, Central London bus guide). The game does not use the fox's scream, which belongs mostly to winter (NHM).
- **Toys for the cubs (the fox's small game, 3.23).** Four things can be carried home: the welly, the chewed ball, Rex's rope toy and the lost teddy. The first three are what the sources name: shoes, balls and dog chews (above); foxes are drawn to dog toys, balls, gardening gloves and worn shoes (Wildlife Trust for Birmingham and the Black Country), and it is the mother who takes shoes, gloves and the like for the cubs to play with (Sussex Wildlife Trust, "Fox cubs in the garden"). The teddy stands for children's toys left out, which a council page lists among things foxes carry off (Renfrewshire Council, Foxes); no source names a teddy as such. Read through search results. The play is the game's own: one toy in her mouth at a time, dropping it at a fright (Rex's bark, the cat's swipe, a near miss on the road), and a cub popping out to take it.

### Up the Thames (added in 3.20): a painted lady, *Vanessa cardui*, in 3D
Sources read through search results, not fetched directly.
- Painted ladies reach Britain each summer from North Africa, the Middle East and southern Europe, and "cannot survive our winter in any form" (The Wildlife Trusts, Painted lady; Butterfly Conservation; UKBMS).
- The yearly round trip runs from tropical Africa to the Arctic Circle and back, about 9,000 miles (Butterfly Conservation, reported by BirdGuides), over up to six generations and 60 degrees of latitude (Stefanescu et al. 2013, *Ecography* 36: 474–486).

- Caterpillars feed mostly on thistles (Butterfly Conservation; Field Studies Council; The Wildlife Trusts, which add mallows and viper's-bugloss). Adults are frequent in gardens and feed on buddleia and other flowers (The Wildlife Trusts); thistles, brambles, knapweeds and clover too (Field Studies Council). The planters of mixed flowers are scenery.
- The Australian painted lady's hindwing eyespots have blue centres in at least three of the four; *V. cardui* has a few tiny pupils or, more often, none. The difference is usual, not absolute (Wikipedia, Australian painted lady; butterflyhouse.com.au). The same drawing and model serve both, with the spots changed.
- Wingspan 58–74 mm (The Wildlife Trusts; Kent Wildlife Trust): Real size shows 65 mm.
- No bird is shown hunting her: no source was found for one. Pretend: the rings, the four sips before she will lay, three eggs, and the balloon.

## Cheeky but true (added in 3.18)
One or two small true things added to each Perth level, with shorter goals. The page text of these sources was read through search results, not fetched directly.
- **Echidna** (bull ant level): echidnas blow bubbles of mucus from the nose; the bubbles burst over the nose tip and wet it, and the evaporation cools their blood (Cooper & Withers 2023, *Biology Letters* 19(1): 20220495; Curtin University media release, "Study finds blowing bubbles among echidna's tricks to beat the heat"). "Snot" is the game's word for mucus. Echidna scats are long dry cylinders, mostly soil and the undigested exoskeletons of ants and termites (University of Adelaide, EchidnaCSI scat identification card; Perry et al. 2022, *Frontiers in Microbiology* 13: 687115). Echidnas mostly eat other ants and termites and attack bull ant nests only now and then (Australian Geographic; University of Queensland news, March 2022); the game's echidna was already there.
- **Queen ant**: mated queens chew off their own wings (NHM, "Flying ant day"; CSIRO, Formicidae). The game says "snaps off", and "Snap!".
- **Blue-banded bee**: blue-banded bees bang their heads on the flower's anthers at about 350 times a second to shake the pollen out (Switzer, Hogendoorn, Ravi & Combes 2016, *Arthropod-Plant Interactions* 10: 1–8, "Shakers and head bangers", measured on *Amegilla murrayensis*; Harvard Gazette, February 2016). The game says "up to 350 times a second". Cuckoo bees (*Thyreus*) lay their eggs in the nests of blue-banded bees, and the cuckoo's larva eats the stored food (Australian Museum, "Cuckoo bees"); the south-west's species is the Waroona cuckoo bee, *Thyreus waroonensis* (Australian Faunal Directory; Friends of Queens Park Bushland). In the game she is chased off by flying at her, and costs one load of pollen if she gets in: the game's own.
- **Bush fly's cow**: in cows, burps can account for about 95% of the methane they give off (CSIRO Double Helix, "Burp! Excuse us"). The game says "nearly all of it comes out as burps".
- **Caterpillar**: the pellets left behind as it eats are shown but no fact goes with them (no source was found that describes them for *Vanessa kershawi*).
- **Termite**: microbes in a termite's gut make methane as they break down wood; termites give off about 1–3% of the world's methane (University of Melbourne, Pursuit, "How a termite's mound filters methane", on a 2018 *PNAS* study; Global Methane Budget, about 9 of 576 Tg a year). "Fart" is the game's word. In lower termites such as *Coptotermes*, nestmates take a drop of hindgut fluid from another's rear end, which passes on the gut microbes they need, and must do so again after each moult (reviews of proctodeal trophallaxis in lower termites: NSF public access repository item 10466916; PMC5390826).
- **Snail**: the anus opens just behind the breathing pore, in the mantle at the edge of the shell (Missouri Department of Conservation, land snails and slugs; Carnegie Museum of Natural History, land snail terminology), and the droppings can look like "a tiny folded rope" (Carnegie Museum of Natural History, "Land snail ecology: diet and behavior").
- **Dragonfly nymph**: already said it breathes with gills inside its rear end and jets by squirting water out (Royal BC Museum, dragonfly larvae); the bubbles and the "Pfft!" are the game's picture of that. A tadpole counting as two meals is the game's own.
- **Blue skimmer**: males of *Orthetrum caledonicum* defend small territories at a pond against other males (Alcock 1988, *Odonatologica* 17(1): 1–8).
- **Mosquito**: mosquitoes find people by the carbon dioxide they breathe out, then body heat and skin odour (Cameron Webb, University of Sydney, via SBS and RNZ). In field traps *Culex quinquefasciatus* was drawn to human foot odour (Mboera, Takken & Sambu 2000, *Bulletin of Entomological Research* 90: 155–159). 
- **Peacock spider, female**: in the close relative *Maratus volans*, a female who waves her abdomen from side to side is signalling that the male has little chance (Girard, Elias & Kasumovic 2015, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 282: 20152222; UNSW news, December 2015). Not studied in *M. speciosus*, so the game says "in a close cousin".
- **Orb web**: small *Argyrodes* (dewdrop spiders; the Australian Museum's name for the usual one is the quicksilver spider, *A. antipodianus*) live in golden orb-weavers' webs and steal small prey (Australian Museum, golden orb weaving spiders; *Argyrodes* page). Tapping it away is the game's own.
- **Frogs**: the western banjo frog's call is a single explosive "bonk" (WA Museum FrogWatch). The speech bubbles are the game's own. Silent "sneak" males are known in the quacking frog of the south-west, not in the motorbike frog, so the game has none.
- The game's own, with no fact claimed: a caterpillar and a tadpole counting double, the stung caterpillar's thrashing, Boing! for springtails, the sandhoppers that hop out of an eaten pile of wrack (they do shelter in it), the gull's splat, "Pooh! Smelly feet!", the crowd and the judges at the Olympics.
- Goals are shorter: ant 8 loads, bee 3 cells, caterpillar 9 munches, butterfly 6 eggs, termite 3 and 3, snail 8, spiderling 6, spider 5 flies, nymph 6, dragonfly 15, female skimmer 6 mosquitoes and 5 spots, sandhopper 7, frog 6 calls, sugar ant mound 12 crumbs with jaws that hold 6.

- **Midges** (Chironomidae) over Lake Joondalup: they swarm from Perth's shallow lakes in spring and summer and come to lights (City of Wanneroo, "Midge information" and its Lake Joondalup treatment; City of Joondalup). Not shown at Herdsman Lake: no source placed them there.
- **Seven-spot ladybird** in Regent's Park: the UK's most common ladybird, in parks, gardens and towns (Woodland Trust); ladybirds are named on Regent's Park planting (Royal Parks), without the species.
- **Red admiral** and **peacock** butterflies on the buddleia: in July and August peacocks, red admirals, small tortoiseshells and commas often gather on buddleia (Butterfly Conservation; Woodland Trust; RSPB).
- **Dung beetles** on the deer droppings in Richmond Park: about 75 species recorded from dung there, among them *Aphodius zenkeri*, tied to deer dung and seldom found outside deer parks (Richmond Park SSSI citation, hosted by the Friends of Richmond Park). Drawn as specks.
- **Large yellow underwing** (*Noctua pronuba*), the moths at the fox's street lamps: common and widespread, gathering at light in numbers (Butterfly Conservation; The Wildlife Trusts). UK-wide; nothing London-specific was found.

## Simplifications (allowed)
- Bugs are drawn far larger than real size relative to predators and plants.
- Time is compressed: a few minutes stands in for days of foraging.
- Ant workers carry one prey at a time; real ants sometimes cooperate.
- The bee mission plays a female; the male sleeping fact is shown at the end.
- Sun patches dry the snail out in seconds; really it takes much longer.
- Termite workers carry one piece at a time to a small mound; real colonies are huge.
- Every bug can escape the tank the same way (through a gap in the lid) at a similar speed, so slow bugs like nymphs aren't stuck.
- Predators only visit now and then (about once a minute) and leave after about 20 seconds.
- Bush fly eggs hatch in seconds in the game; really it takes hours.
- The bush fly can sit on a cow or the kangaroo and be carried across the paddock. Real bush flies land on animals for moisture and protein, not for transport, and take off when the animal moves; the only real "ride" is on the spring winds.
- The Bug Olympics are pretend games played with real abilities: a sandhopper's jump, a dragonfly's dart, a dung beetle's burying, an ant's carrying and a bee's buzz. The long-jump pit sits in a stadium, the rings and the clock are the game's, three jumps are allowed, and a cow pat is buried in seconds rather than hours. The medal thresholds are the game's own.
- The frog chorus: all four frogs call on one spring night in the game; the moaning frog really calls in autumn and early winter. The calls are synthesised from oscillators, not recordings. The chorus takes turns in a neat round of four with one gap; real choruses are messier. The female hops straight to you within a minute.
- Predators give up a chase in the safe home area, and pause before chasing so young players can react.
- The nymph stage is a few minutes; real nymphs take months.
- Spider jumps follow a target that hops mid-air; real jumps are ballistic.
- Emergence happens on the water's surface in the game; in reality the nymph climbs well up the stem first.
- The day lasts 10 minutes; at night every bug sleeps. Really, sandhoppers, mosquitoes and many others are active at night.
- The sandhopper's beach is shown at dusk; the 2D gull dives and the plover gives up after a few seconds.
- Mosquito bite spots are marked; people only wave the mosquito away when looking around, and only if she is in the red zone that shows where they're looking (close round their face, and a wedge that sweeps across in front of them; the zone is the game's own). There is time to fly out of it first, and a wave just blows her a little way off (no heart lost). In the London café the hand's reach is shown as a red zone the same way.
- The 3D butterfly flies at a steady speed and lands automatically on a flower's ring.
- The female peacock spider: five or six males a round, at most three at once; each dances for 10 to 20 seconds, gives up and walks off, and comes back later. Any choice is fine in the game. Eggs hatch 18 seconds after the sac is spun (really about two weeks, and the spiderlings stay about two more), and an ant that reaches the sac takes one egg. **A new mate for each brood is pretend**: real mated females almost never mate again (see the section above), and the game says so.
- The male's courtship is ten beats in twelve seconds with the moves in a fixed order; real displays go on for minutes and vary. The drumming is drawn as rings on the sand. She steps closer or away to show how it is going; really she simply stays, walks off or attacks.
- The suitors differ in size, fan colour (eight shades of the species' blue-green and orange), tempo and dance more than real males of one species do, so a child can tell them apart; all are *M. speciosus*. Each round has one standout with a vivid fan, a steady quick beat and the complete display (fan, leg wave and drumming together). He is not bigger than the others: no study shows females prefer bigger males.
- The garden: animals arrive within seconds of the right plants going in; really it takes seasons. Visitors' needs are simplified rules.
- The orb web: threads must attach to branches or threads, but there is no tension physics; the spiral is laid automatically.
- The ant nest: one ant digs the whole nest; really thousands of workers share the work. Floods wash you out rather than drowning you.
- The family tree is simplified: only the bugs in the game are shown, and springtails and other branches are left out.
- Real size scales the whole drawing (antennae, legs or open wings) to the published body length or wingspan, and the screen's scale is a guess until a coin is matched.
